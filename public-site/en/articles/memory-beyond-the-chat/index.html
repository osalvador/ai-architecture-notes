Context Store: preserving system intent and knowledge

Connecting specifications, tests and architectural checks in a versioned record that helps people understand and evolve a system.

The idea in one sentence

Section titled “The idea in one sentence”

Agents can produce code quickly while leaving behind an understanding of why the system is built that way. Stella Berhe, Stephan Bragner, Vikram Maran and Anand Jayaraman propose preserving that knowledge in versioned artefacts available to people and agents.

Their Context Store is a shared record of intent, behaviour and architectural conformance. Its source of truth is maintained artefacts alongside code, rather than a model’s private memory or a document written once.

The problem they address

Section titled “The problem they address”

The text describes a gap between generating functionality and understanding how it fits the system. A prototype may compile and appear finished while integration, behavioural cases, performance constraints and inherited decisions remain unresolved. Without recorded reasons, the next agent or developer has to rediscover them, or may create a locally working change that weakens the architecture.

The authors connect this with evolutionary architecture: systems change, so important boundaries need to be observable and verifiable during change. Understanding becomes a property to maintain throughout the lifecycle.

Three practices build the Context Store

Section titled “Three practices build the Context Store”

The proposal combines three disciplines rather than depending on a new tool:

Specifications alongside code

Section titled “Specifications alongside code”

A specification expresses purpose, scope, constraints and acceptance criteria. It lives in the repository and is reviewed like code, serving both as an assignment for the agent and a reference for the reviewer.

It evolves when reality and intent diverge. Creating it to guide an agent and then abandoning it produces stale and misleading context.

Tests before accepting generated code

Section titled “Tests before accepting generated code”

With TDD, a checkable behaviour is defined first; the test fails until the implementation meets it. The test both communicates an expectation and provides evidence about the output.

Human judgement remains necessary to choose relevant behaviour and failure cases. A green suite can confirm the wrong interpretation when requirements or tests were poorly formulated.

Fitness functions for architectural properties

Section titled “Fitness functions for architectural properties”

Fitness functions are executable checks on properties the team wants to preserve, such as modularity, performance, security, observability or deployability. The article proposes integrating them into CI to detect a change violating an agreed constraint before merging.

The authors suggest prioritising costly architectural problems and automating checks that can be expressed reliably. The team maintains this catalogue; stale rules or rules treated as noise stop being useful sensors.

What is recorded?

Section titled “What is recorded?”

The article describes four layers:

  • Structure: the system’s parts and their relationships.
  • Lineage: reasoning and decisions explaining the design.
  • Behaviour: expectations and behaviour observed through tests.
  • Conformance: active architectural constraints and whether they hold.

These support views for people seeking an explanation, agents preparing changes and CI processes checking constraints. The same base supports review before deployment and investigation or evolution afterwards.

A bounded starting point

Section titled “A bounded starting point”

The article proposes selecting one feature and:

  1. Adding a reviewable specification to the repository.
  2. Writing or approving a failing test before accepting generated code.
  3. Identifying the riskiest architectural properties and making priority checks blocking in CI.
  4. Updating artefacts when intent or the system changes.

The change then leaves a useful trace for the next cycle. The specification guides inputs; tests and controls provide output signals.

What the article does not establish

Section titled “What the article does not establish”

The framework assumes a versioned repository, a custom development environment, an executable specification and CI. The authors exclude no-code platforms and packaged products. A small team with one service may use only part of the approach if one person can still understand the system.

The article cites previous productivity and stability research and an SDD experience in banking, acknowledging that one case cannot support generalisation. It does not comparatively evaluate the complete Context Store across organisations; it presents a reasoned framework and practices rather than a universally demonstrated impact formula.

Artefacts alone are insufficient. Specifications can misstate intent, tests can omit relevant cases and rules can misrepresent current boundaries. The record helps only when consulted, checked and maintained.

Connection with other readings

Section titled “Connection with other readings”

Ajay Prakash’s LinkedIn presentation asks how an agent retrieves task procedures and tools. Its playbooks and MCP tools provide organisational context on demand; Context Store focuses on preserving intent, behaviour and constraints over time.

Their combination is an editorial hypothesis, rather than a conclusion shared by the authors: an agent may need a current procedure to investigate a service and versioned contracts explaining what must remain intact. The related synthesis develops this distinction and its conditions; the decision guide applies it to a fictional case.

My reading

Section titled “My reading”

The compelling part is linking documentation and verification. A written decision explains the system; a test and automated rule can reveal departures from intent. Together they could preserve more useful knowledge than an isolated explanation outside the change workflow.

I would avoid turning every preference into a CI control. Value appears when the team can identify the risk an artefact addresses, its maintainer and the action triggered by change. An unused artefact or irrelevant check adds effort and false confidence.

My question for each assisted change is: what persistent evidence will let someone else understand intent and check behaviour without reconstructing everything? It connects the LinkedIn procedures with the fictional case in the pilot guide.
